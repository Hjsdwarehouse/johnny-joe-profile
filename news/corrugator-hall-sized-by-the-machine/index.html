A corrugator hall is sized by the machine, not by the pallets

A packaging plant that adds a production line and doubles its floor area is not really doubling a building.

A packaging plant that adds a production line and doubles its floor area is not really doubling a building. It is building one hall around one machine, and then filling whatever is left with paper.

That distinction decides almost everything. The machine sets a clear span, a height, a floor tolerance and a structural support line. The pallets, the reels and the finished board fill the space afterwards, and they are the part that can be changed later. Get the machine hall right and the rest is fit-out. Get it wrong and the machine is installed in a building that constrains it for as long as it runs.

1. The machine sets the span; the pallets fill what is left

A corrugator is long, it is fixed to the floor at defined points, and it needs clear space on both sides for reels in and board out. The working rule I start with is to set the span from the machine plus its two service aisles, and then check whether the resulting bay also works for the storage that follows.

In practice that produces a main bay in the order of 18 to 24 m (60 to 80 ft) clear, with the corrugator running along the long axis. Anything narrower forces a column line down one side of the machine, which is survivable but constrains the reel handling for the life of the building.

The second decision is height. Not for the machine — for the ducting and the reel crane. A hall with 8 m (26 ft) to eaves will often deliver around 6.5 m (21 ft) of usable clearance once the duct and the crane rail are in, and that difference is the difference between a workable reel store and one that is stacked two high by hand.

2. The column line nobody wants

Every client asks for a column-free hall. What they usually need is a column-free band around the machine, and columns are acceptable everywhere else.

That is worth arguing for explicitly rather than discovering at tender. A fully clear span across a large packaging hall is achievable but it is paid for in rafter depth, in crane capacity during erection and in the foundation. A grid that keeps one wide bay along the machine and uses a normal grid either side gives the same operational result for a materially lighter frame.

The honest version of this conversation happens at concept stage. It does not happen after the drawing has been issued.

3. Paper is a humidity product

Board stock, reels and finished boxes all take up and give off moisture. That makes the building a humidity envelope as much as a weather envelope, and it changes two things.

First, the roof. A metal roof with no controlled internal face will condense on the underside whenever the outside temperature drops faster than the inside air can follow, and the water lands on the stock directly below. That is why a liner — continuous, with taped joints — is not a finishing item in a packaging plant. It is a process requirement, and the detail at the eaves and at the wall junction decides whether it works.

Second, the floor slab moisture. Board stacked on a slab that is still giving off moisture will pick it up from below. The drying period before handover is part of the building programme, not an inconvenience to be compressed.

4. The floor: flat, level and able to take a moving load

A corrugator needs a floor that is flat and level over its length, because the machine is aligned to the floor and shims only go so far.

The practical tolerances are the ones worth writing into the brief: flatness under the machine base, a defined fall in the wet end and in the vehicle areas, and a slab designed for the actual wheel loads of loaded clamp trucks rather than for a nominal warehouse load. Clamp trucks are heavy at the front and they turn tightly, and the corner of a slab joint is where that shows up first.

In an extension, the floor is also the physical connection to the old building. Two slabs poured years apart will move differently, and a joint that is filled flush to be truck-friendly will crack unless it is a real joint with a real load transfer detail.

5. Extending while the line keeps running

A live packaging plant cannot stop for a year, so most extensions are built beside the running hall and connected in a short window.

That has three consequences for the steel design. The new frame should be stable on its own, so it does not depend on the old building before the connection is made. The connection itself should be a small, defined package of work that can be done in one planned stop. And the new roof should be closed and the new floor finished before the opening is cut, so the running plant is never exposed to weather.

It also means the extension has to be drawn around the existing services, not over them. A new column line landing on a cable trench is the most common avoidable delay in this kind of project.

6. What I want before drawings

  • The machine: footprint, foundation points, service aisles, and the height of everything above it.
  • Reel store and finished goods volumes, and how they are handled — clamp truck, forklift, or crane.
  • The vehicle turning circle and the dock arrangement, because they set the slab and the door positions.
  • Whether a second line is planned, and on which side of the first.
  • The existing building's structure and services, at the wall where the extension will connect.
  • The wind speed to be used, taken from the applicable code at the project site rather than a round number, together with the roof fixing pattern — a large, low, wide hall is dominated by uplift at the edges and corners, and that is a fixing question more often than a frame question.

A packaging hall is a machine building with a warehouse attached. Draw it in that order and the extension is straightforward. Draw it as a warehouse and the machine will spend its life in a compromise.
