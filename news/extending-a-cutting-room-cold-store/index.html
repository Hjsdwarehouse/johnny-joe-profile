Extending a cutting room: the cold store is the easy part

When a processor decides to enlarge a cutting room by roughly a third and add two new cold rooms, the conversation always starts with the steel and it should start somewhere else.

When a processor decides to enlarge a cutting room by roughly a third and add two new cold rooms, the conversation always starts with the steel and it should start somewhere else.

The steel is the part that is easy to price and easy to draw. The part that decides whether the extension works is the sequence: what the new cold rooms have to hold, at what temperature, for how long, and what the existing building will do to them once they are attached to it.

An extension on a live meat plant is not a building project with a plant in the way. It is a cold-chain project that happens to need a frame around it.

1. An extension is a cold-chain decision before it is a steel decision

The first question is not area. It is dwell time.

A cutting room that produces fresh product and ships it the same day needs a chiller sized for hours. The same room producing vacuum-packed cuts for a distributor who collects twice a week needs a chiller sized for days, and a freezer behind it. Those two briefs produce completely different buildings at the same footprint: different insulation thickness, different door type, different floor, different refrigeration plant, and a different structural grid to suit the racking or the rails.

So the useful first move is to write down, for each new room: product in, product out, temperature band, how long anything sits, and how it gets in and out — pallet, trolley, or overhead rail. Three of those five answers usually contradict the drawing that was already circulated.

2. Where the new box meets the old wall

The junction between the existing plant and the new cold volume is where most extensions leak, and it leaks in a way that takes a year to notice.

The new building will move. It is warmer on one side, colder on the other, it is founded on its own slab, and it is much stiffer than the wall it is bolted to. If the two are tied rigidly, the movement goes into the panels. If they are separated, someone has to detail the gap — and the gap is where warm, humid plant air reaches a cold surface and condenses.

What works is a deliberate separation with a properly formed closure: a movement joint that is wide enough to actually be a joint, an insulated transition that keeps the cold face cold and the warm face warm, and a vapour-tight layer that is continuous across the junction rather than stopped at each side of it. This is a detail, not a concept — which is exactly why it gets left to the erection crew.

3. The floor carries the whole argument

In a meat plant, the floor is the building.

Three things have to be settled together and are usually settled by three different people. The insulated slab: where the insulation sits, how the slab is protected from frost heave under a freezer, and how the thermal break runs at the perimeter so the cold does not travel out into the yard. The falls: one consistent direction per room, into gullies that are reachable and cleanable, with a fall steep enough that water actually moves — in the order of 1 in 100 over the room rather than a token tilt. And the washdown regime, which decides the kerbs, the coving and whether a forklift will eventually take the corner off every upstand it can reach.

Do the floor once. A cold room floor is the only part of the building that cannot be revised while the plant is running.

4. Chiller and freezer are two different buildings under one roof

It is tempting to treat a chiller and a freezer as the same volume with a different thermostat. They behave differently in almost every respect that matters to a steel building.

A freezer wants a continuous insulated envelope, a heated or thermally broken floor, doors that are opened rarely and closed reliably, and as little penetration through the envelope as possible. A chiller tolerates more: more door traffic, more openings, a lighter envelope, and — importantly — a much higher internal humidity, which is where the condensation problems start on any cold surface the insulation missed.

Putting them side by side and sharing a wall means that wall has a different job on each face. That has to be a designed assembly, not the same panel used twice.

5. Building a cold room around a running plant

The programme is a hygiene problem before it is a construction problem.

An extension that opens a wall into a production area creates a route for dust, birds, water and people who should not be there. The practical answer is to sequence the work so that the new volume is closed and sealed before the opening is cut, and to treat that opening as a controlled event with a defined start and end — temporary enclosure, a defined cleaning step before production resumes, and a single person responsible for the seal.

It also means the steel frame is erected from outside the existing wall line wherever possible. A frame designed to be built from the inside, in a plant that cannot stop, is a frame that will be built badly.

6. What I want before drawings

  • Product, temperature band and dwell time for each new room, written separately.
  • How product crosses the boundary between old and new, at what height, and how often.
  • The existing floor build-up and where its drainage runs — before anything is cut.
  • Whether the new rooms will ever run colder than they do on day one.
  • The washdown method and the chemicals, because they decide the floor and every fixing in it.
  • The wind speed to be used, taken from the applicable code at the project site rather than a round number, together with the roof edge detail — on a low building with a large flat roof, the edge and the fixings are what fail first in a storm, not the frame.

An extension is decided by the cold side. The steel just has to be drawn after those answers exist, not before.
