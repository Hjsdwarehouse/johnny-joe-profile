A lubricant and chemical store is a fire plan with a roof on it

A store for oils, lubricants and chemicals is usually drawn as a shed with racks, and priced as a shed with racks.

A store for oils, lubricants and chemicals is usually drawn as a shed with racks, and priced as a shed with racks. Then someone asks how the compartments work, and a building designed around a floor area turns out to be one that should have been designed around a fire boundary.

That is the reversal worth making early, because in this building type the plan is a fire strategy first. The steel is the outer skin, and the outer skin is the easy part.

1. The plan is a fire strategy before it is a floor plan

What goes into a chemical store determines how the store has to be divided. Products that can burn, products that can ignite at low temperature, products that react with water, and products that give off vapour heavier than air all want different treatment, and no single open hall serves all of them well.

The first useful exercise is not a layout, it is a list: what is stored, in what quantity, in what packaging, and what happens if two of those groups meet. Lubricating oil in drums and solvents in cans do not belong in the same compartment, and neither belongs next to anything that oxidises.

Once those groups are known, the store divides itself into compartments, and the compartment layout becomes the drawing that everything else follows — the doors, the drainage, the ventilation, the rack positions and the structural grid.

2. Compartment size, and what actually decides it

Compartment size is normally quoted as an area or a volume, and it is easy to focus on that number while missing what sets it.

What sets it, in practice, is the distance from the furthest point in the compartment to an exit, and the separation between one hazard group and the next. A small compartment with a long internal travel is worse than a larger one with a clear route out, and a compartment that is subdivided by racks so that the route out is a maze is worse than either.

Two layout consequences follow. Racks should be arranged so that a person can walk out in a straight line from the far corner, which usually means orienting rack runs along the exit axis rather than across it. And the doors between compartments need to be in the right place for the way the store is actually worked, not just for the way it is drawn — a compartment door that the forklift cannot reach without a three-point turn is a door that will be propped open.

3. Containment: the floor is the second wall

For a store holding liquids, the floor is a containment structure, and it is the part most often under-designed.

Three things have to be true at once. The floor has to fall to a collection point, so a spill goes somewhere rather than spreading under the racks. The collection point has to be able to hold the volume of the largest container plus whatever else drains into it, and it must be emptyable without a tanker blocking the exit. And the floor finish has to resist whatever is stored, because a floor that is attacked by the product is a floor that becomes permeable exactly when it matters.

The detail that gets left off is the threshold. A containment kerb at every door, and around every opening, is what keeps a spill inside the room it happened in. A store with a sloped floor and an open door at the low corner discharges into the yard.

4. Ventilation, and the pressure that keeps vapour where it belongs

Vapour from oils and solvents is usually heavier than air, which means it collects at floor level and travels along the floor to any opening.

That is the opposite of what a conventional high-level extract assumes. A store handling these products needs low-level extract, with make-up air introduced high, so the vapour is drawn out at the level where it accumulates rather than being pushed around the room. Where the store is divided into compartments, each compartment needs its own extract, or the compartment with the strongest extract will pull vapour out of the one next to it.

The other half of this is pressure. A store should sit slightly negative relative to the spaces around it, so air leaks in rather than vapour leaking out. That is a design decision about extract and make-up air balance, and it is worth stating on the drawing, because the site will otherwise install one fan and call it ventilation.

5. Steel in a compartment: outside it, or protected

The steel frame's job in this building is to stay standing long enough for the compartment to do its work.

There are two honest ways to achieve that, and they should be chosen deliberately rather than by default. The frame can be outside the compartment — the structure and the compartment separated, so the wall that bounds the hazard is not the structure that carries the roof. Or the frame can be inside, with its exposed members given the protection that the compartment requires.

The first is usually cheaper in a new building and much harder to achieve in an extension, which is why it should be decided at concept stage. The second creates a maintenance obligation that runs for the life of the building: protection that is damaged by a forklift and not repaired is protection that no longer exists, and nobody inspects it because it is above eye level.

Where the compartment wall meets the steel, the junction is the weak point. A wall that stops at the underside of a purlin, with a gap for the purlin to pass through, is a wall with a hole in it.

6. What I want before drawings

  • The product groups, quantities and packaging, and which groups must not share a compartment.
  • How the store is worked: who goes in, how often, with what equipment.
  • The largest single container, for containment volume.
  • Whether the floor will be washed, and with what.
  • Whether the frame is intended to be outside the compartments or protected inside them.
  • The wind speed to be used, taken from the applicable code at the project site rather than a round number, plus the door and louvre detail — a store with large extract openings is loaded inside as well as outside, and the fixings around those openings are where a storm finds the weakness.

A chemical store is not a shed with racks in it. It is a set of compartments that happen to be inside a steel envelope, and the envelope is the part you can change later.
