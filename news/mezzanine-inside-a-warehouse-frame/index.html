A mezzanine inside a warehouse: the decision is whether to touch the frame

A mezzanine is sold as extra floor area, and it is priced per square metre like a floor.

A mezzanine is sold as extra floor area, and it is priced per square metre like a floor. The structural question that actually matters is asked much less often: is this platform part of the building, or a separate structure standing inside it?

The answer changes the columns, the connections, the vibration behaviour and, eventually, whether the mezzanine can be modified without touching the roof. It should be decided at concept stage, because retrofitting the decision is expensive.

1. Connected or independent: the first fork in the road

A mezzanine tied into the main frame shares its columns and its stability, and it transfers its loads through members that were designed for something else.

That can be entirely sound — a portal frame with spare capacity and a column line in the right place will happily carry a light platform. It becomes unsound when the platform's bracing conflicts with the frame's bracing, when the tie forces a frame to resist movement it was not designed for, or when the platform is stiff enough to take load that the frame was meant to take.

An independent platform avoids all of that: its own columns, its own bracing, its own foundations, standing clear of the frame with a movement gap at the roof and wall junctions. It costs more steel and more foundations, and it buys the freedom to change it later without touching the building.

My working rule: if the platform is permanent, light and aligned with the frame's grid, connect it. If it is heavy, likely to change, or fighting the grid, keep it independent.

2. Vibration: the criterion nobody puts on the drawing

A mezzanine floor is usually a steel deck on beams, and steel decks are lively.

The problem is not strength. It is that a platform carrying people, or a platform carrying a forklift, will deflect and rebound in a way people notice — and once people notice, the platform is regarded as unsafe even when it is not. A mezzanine that bounces when someone walks past the coffee machine is a mezzanine that gets a bad reputation it cannot lose.

The design answer is stiffness, not strength: deeper beams, shorter spans, or a concrete topping to add mass and damping. As a working figure, I want the first natural frequency of a walking-loaded platform checked and kept comfortably above the pace of walking — in the order of 3 Hz and above for ordinary foot traffic, higher where the platform carries rhythmic activity.

That check costs nothing at design stage and is very expensive afterwards, because fixing it means adding steel under a floor that is already in use.

3. Columns that avoid the racking, not the grid

Inside an existing warehouse, the mezzanine's columns have to miss the racking, the aisle, and the forklift's turning circle.

That usually means the mezzanine's grid is driven by the rack layout rather than by a structural module, and the spans become irregular. Irregular spans are fine structurally, but they force attention onto the connections: a beam that frames into a column at a different depth on each side needs a detail that accommodates it, and that detail is where fabrication errors appear.

The other constraint is the base. A mezzanine column standing on an existing slab imposes a point load the slab may not have been designed for, and the fix is either a spreader plate, a new pad cut into the slab, or a column taken down to a new foundation. Deciding that before the columns are located avoids discovering it after the racking is installed.

4. Fire, egress and the floor above

A mezzanine introduces a floor above the working area, and that changes the building's behaviour in a fire.

Two things follow immediately. The structure above has to remain stable long enough for people to leave, which for an unprotected steel platform means either protecting the steel or accepting a short travel distance and a fast route out. And the space below the mezzanine becomes a room, which means it needs its own escape route that does not pass through the space above.

The detail that gets forgotten is the edge. A mezzanine edge is an opening in the floor, and the openings — the stair, the lift, the pallet gate, the gap where a conveyor passes — are where smoke travels vertically. A platform with an unprotected stair opening in the middle defeats the compartment logic of the whole building.

5. Access, and the stair that takes a bay

The stair is a structure, and it always lands where something else was going to be.

A stair serving a 4 m (13 ft) high platform needs a run in the order of 6 to 7 m (20 to 23 ft) with landings, which is a bay of floor area on both levels. Placing it at the end of a rack run rather than in the middle, and arranging it so that the stair's structure is independent of the platform's, avoids the situation where the stair's bracing and the platform's bracing disagree.

Goods access is a separate question. A pallet gate or a lift is often needed, and a lift shaft inside a warehouse is a structure with its own foundations and its own fire separation. If a lift is even possible, leave a bay for it.

6. What I want before drawings

  • The load: people, storage, or vehicles, and the worst combination.
  • Whether the platform must be modifiable later.
  • The racking and aisle layout it has to fit between.
  • The existing slab, its reinforcement and what it can take as a point load.
  • The stair and lift positions, and the escape route from below.
  • The wind speed to be used, taken from the applicable code at the project site rather than a round number — even an internal platform matters if it is tied to a frame that carries wind, because the tie changes how that frame behaves under load.

A mezzanine is cheap floor area until it starts arguing with the building. Decide early whether it is part of the frame or a guest inside it, and the rest follows.
