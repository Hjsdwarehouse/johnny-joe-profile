A pipe rack: the towers are the building, the rack is the frame

A pipe rack gets drawn as a series of frames carrying pipes, and then someone asks how a person reaches the third level, and the drawing changes shape.

A pipe rack gets drawn as a series of frames carrying pipes, and then someone asks how a person reaches the third level, and the drawing changes shape.

Access is not an accessory on a rack. On a fuel terminal it is the thing that decides whether the rack can be maintained at all, and it is usually the last item designed and the first item value-engineered. A rack with three levels and no tower is a rack that gets climbed with a ladder and a harness, which means it gets inspected less often than it should.

1. Vertical access sets the plan before the pipes do

The tower position is a plan decision, and it constrains everything else on the rack.

Two rules cover most cases. Every point on every level should be within a short walk of a tower — practically, the far end of a level should be no more than a few bays away, so that a person carrying a tool does not cross half the terminal to reach a flange. And the tower should be reachable from ground level without crossing a pipe route, because a staircase that lands in the middle of a live line is a staircase nobody uses in an emergency.

That usually means towers at intervals along the rack, and it means the rack's structural bays should be laid out with the tower spacings in mind rather than the other way round. A tower dropped into a rack afterwards forces a bay to be re-detailed, and the detail is rarely as good as it would have been at concept stage.

2. Stairs, ladders and the honest choice between them

A caged vertical ladder is cheaper than a staircase, and it is the wrong answer for the main access on a terminal where people carry equipment.

The working distinction is purpose. Ladders suit occasional inspection by one person with nothing in their hands. Stairs suit routine access, carrying, and evacuation. A rack that is inspected weekly, with sampling or valve work, wants stairs; a rack that carries a single line to a seldom-used tie-in can live with ladders.

The consequence for the structure is significant: a staircase tower is a braced steel structure with its own foundations, often 4 to 6 m (13 to 20 ft) square, standing beside the rack. A ladder needs a small platform and a cage. Choosing stairs across a terminal adds real structure, and it is better to know that at concept stage than to discover it when the towers appear on the drawing.

3. Crossing a road: the portal that carries the rack

Where a rack crosses a road, it becomes a portal, and a portal is a different structure from the frames either side.

The span is set by the road width plus a margin for the kerb and for anything that might be added later, and the clearance is set by the tallest vehicle plus a margin for resurfacing. Both of those numbers are usually larger than the rack's normal bay spacing, which means the portal frames are deeper and the foundations heavier than their neighbours.

Two things go wrong. The portal is designed as a frame but the rack continues on both sides as a braced structure, so the two systems meet at a joint that transfers forces nobody calculated. And the road is widened later — a second lane, a turning circle for a longer vehicle — and the portal that was adequate becomes a pinch point.

Setting the portal span one bay wider than the current road is cheap insurance, and it also gives the drainage and the kerb somewhere to live.

4. Pipes move, and the rack has to allow it

A pipe rack is a structure that holds things which expand, contract, vibrate and occasionally move sideways.

Thermal movement along a long line is real, and it has to be allowed for at defined locations rather than distributed at every support. A line that is restrained at every frame will push its movement into whichever frame is stiffest, and the result is a deformed rack and a failed anchor rather than a controlled slide.

The design decision is where the line is anchored and where it slides, and it has to be made with the piping layout in hand, not assumed by the structural designer. The structural consequence is that some frames carry longitudinal load and others must not, which changes the bracing pattern along the rack.

Vertical movement matters too: a line that is hot in service and cold in shutdown will lift off a support or press down on it, and a rack designed only for the operating case will show it at the sliding shoes.

5. Cable and pipe on the same rack

Most terminals run cable tray on the pipe rack, usually on the top level or on a cantilevered bracket, and this is where electrical and structural decisions collide.

The tray needs to be reachable, separated from hot lines, and routed so that it does not pass through the space where a flange has to be broken for maintenance. That last requirement is the one that gets ignored: a tray directly above a flange is a tray that has to be unbolted to change a gasket.

Cantilever brackets are also the most abused part of a rack. They get loaded with trays, then with a second tray, then with a conduit run, and the bracket was sized for one. Designing them with a stated allowance, and marking the allowance on the drawing, is the cheapest way to prevent that.

6. What I want before drawings

  • The piping layout with line sizes, temperatures and which lines are anchored.
  • How often each level is accessed, and by how many people, carrying what.
  • Road crossings, their current width and any planned widening.
  • Whether cable tray or conduit will share the rack, and on which level.
  • The largest item that will ever be lifted onto the top level.
  • The wind speed to be used, taken from the applicable code at the project site rather than a round number, plus the open-frame detail — a bare rack has far more exposed surface than its plan suggests, and the towers take the load at the point where the rack is least tied back.

On a pipe rack, the towers are the part people use and the part nobody draws first. Put them on the plan before the pipes, and the terminal works.
