A pump house: corrosion class is a selection input, not a paint choice

A pump house is usually the smallest building on a treatment site and the first one to look tired.

A pump house is usually the smallest building on a treatment site and the first one to look tired.

The reason is not the structure. It is that the building is designed in the abstract — a steel shed, a door, a roof — and then someone asks what finish it should have, at a point where the answer is already constrained by everything that has been drawn. By then the aggressive zone has been built into the layout, the ventilation is a louvre on a wall, and the floor falls the wrong way.

Corrosion class has to be an input to the layout, not a specification applied at the end.

1. Corrosion class is a selection input, not a paint choice

The environment inside a treatment plant building varies enormously over a few metres, and the variation is what matters.

A dry room housing electrical panels and a dosing area with acid or chlorine in the air are not the same environment, and steel in the second will fail much faster than steel in the first regardless of what is painted on it. Adding a heavier coating to the whole building is the expensive way of solving a problem that exists in one corner.

The useful approach is to map the building into exposure zones before the structure is drawn: where the chemicals are, where the wet process is, where the steam or spray is, and where it is simply humid. Then the structure and the finishes can be chosen per zone — hot-dip galvanizing or a coating system selected for the project environment in the aggressive areas, something simpler elsewhere — and the money goes where the risk is.

That mapping also tells you where you should not put steel at all. A wall or a partition that can be a different material is sometimes better than a steel wall with a special coating on it.

2. Where the aggressive zone actually is

The aggressive zone in a treatment building is rarely where people expect.

It is at the floor, not at the roof. Chemical spills, splashes at tank bases, washdown water and the general damp that collects at low level all sit in the bottom metre of the building, and that is where the steel, the fixings and the floor meet. A column base standing in a puddle of slightly acidic water is the most predictable corrosion site in the whole plant.

It is also at the openings. A louvre or a door on the windward side admits not just air but airborne droplets, and everything within a few metres of that opening is in a worse environment than the rest of the room.

And it is under the roof, where condensation forms on the underside of a cold sheet and drips onto whatever is below. In a humid building with a metal roof, that drip is continuous rather than occasional, and it is why a liner or a controlled internal face is often a corrosion decision rather than an aesthetic one.

3. The floor: chemical resistance and fall together

The floor of a pump house or dosing area is asked to do two jobs at once, and they are usually specified separately by different people.

It has to resist the chemicals, which means a finish selected against the actual product rather than a generic "chemical resistant" coating. And it has to fall to a collection point so that a spill or a washdown goes somewhere controlled, in the order of 1 in 100 toward a sump that can be emptied.

Where those two requirements meet, the detail decides whether either works. A fall that leads to a sump in the corner beside a column puts the aggressive liquid exactly where the steel is least protected. Moving the sump to the middle of a bay, or bundling the column base, costs almost nothing at design stage and removes the problem.

The threshold at every door matters for the same reason: without it, containment is theoretical.

4. Ventilation and condensation in a wet room

A wet room with a metal roof will condense on the underside whenever the outside surface is colder than the inside air, which in a treatment plant is most nights.

Two interventions, and they solve different halves of the problem. Ventilation removes the moisture-laden air before it reaches the cold surface, which requires an air change rate matched to the actual evaporation in the room — a louvre and a fan chosen by habit will not do it. And a controlled internal face keeps the cold surface away from the moist air, so condensation forms on a surface that can drain rather than on one that drips.

The combination is what makes a pump house durable. Either alone produces a building that is wet in one season and corroded in the next.

5. Access for maintenance, not for construction

Pump houses are dominated by things that have to be removed: pumps, valves, shafts, motors, and occasionally a whole skid.

That means the building needs a route for the largest item to leave, and it needs to be a route that exists without dismantling the roof. A door sized for a person and a pump house full of equipment is a building that gets cut open once and then has a permanent hole in it.

The practical answer is a removable panel or a large door on one gable, designed as part of the structure rather than added later, plus a lifting point above the largest item. A pump house with a defined lifting beam is a pump house that can be maintained; one without it is a pump house that gets maintained by improvising over a running machine.

6. What I want before drawings

  • What is stored and dosed, in what form, and where the tanks sit on the plan.
  • Where the wet process is, and where splashes and spills are expected.
  • Whether the floor will be washed, and with what.
  • The largest item that will ever be removed from the building.
  • Whether the building will be ventilated mechanically or naturally, and how much vapour the process produces.
  • The wind speed to be used, taken from the applicable code at the project site rather than a round number, plus the louvre and door detail — a building with large openings on a wet, windy site receives water through them as well as wind, and the fixings around those openings fail first.

A pump house is decided by where the aggressive zone is, not by what colour it is painted. Map the exposure first, and the rest of the building follows.
