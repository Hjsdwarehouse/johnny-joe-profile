A weighbridge is a building that trucks hit: design the interface first

The weighbridge house is the smallest building on an industrial site and the one most often rebuilt.

The weighbridge house is the smallest building on an industrial site and the one most often rebuilt.

Not because it falls down. Because it is placed where a loaded truck has to stop, reverse and occasionally misjudge, and the building is the thing that stops it. A weighbridge office that survives ten years without damage is one that was designed around the vehicle's mistakes rather than around the operator's comfort.

1. The approach is the design, not the office

What decides a weighbridge layout is the queue, and the queue is a function of the cycle.

A truck arrives, waits, moves onto the plate, stops, is weighed, gets a ticket and leaves. Each of those steps has a duration, and the total is the cycle time. If the site dispatches more vehicles per hour than the cycle allows, a queue forms — and the queue has to stand somewhere that is not the public road and not the plate.

The practical numbers are worth working out early. A cycle of three to four minutes per truck limits a single weighbridge to roughly fifteen to twenty vehicles an hour, and that is before anything goes wrong. Two bridges side by side do not double the rate unless there is room for two independent approach lanes and two exit lanes, which is where most layouts fail: the second bridge is there, but the queue has nowhere to split.

2. Where the canopy goes, and how long it is

A canopy over a weighbridge exists to keep the operator, the ticket printer and the driver dry, and to stop the plate filling with water.

Its length is set by the vehicles that have to stand under it. A single unit plus a trailer can be 18 m (60 ft) long, and weighing is often done in two passes — axle by axle, or tractor then trailer — so the canopy may have to cover a longer length than the plate itself.

The width follows the lanes plus the operator's position. If the operator has to step out to check a seal or a load, that step has to be under cover and out of the traffic lane, which usually means a raised island with the office on it rather than a cabin at the edge.

Drainage is the detail that decides whether the canopy works. A large roof over a hardstanding collects a lot of water, and it has to leave without crossing the plate area. Gullies in front of the plate rather than behind it, and a fall away from the office door, are the two decisions that prevent a weighbridge from becoming a puddle with a scale in it.

3. The impact interface: bollards, kerbs and a sacrificial skin

Trucks hit weighbridges for a living. The design should assume it.

Three measures cover most of the damage. A line of bollards or a heavy kerb at the corner where the approach turns onto the plate, set back far enough that a misjudged turn hits the kerb rather than the office. A protective barrier between the traffic lane and any structure a person occupies. And a building skin that can be replaced — a sacrificial panel at bumper height is cheaper to renew than a wall that has to be rebuilt.

The kerb and bollard detail matters more than their strength. A bollard cast into an unreinforced island will be pushed out of the ground; one cast into a properly reinforced island, with the load spread into the slab, will stop the vehicle. The difference is in the foundation, not the post.

4. The office: small, hot, and full of equipment

A weighbridge office is a small room with a lot of equipment in it — a terminal, a printer, a screen, possibly a barrier control and a camera recorder — and in a hot climate it becomes a heat problem.

Two things follow. The building needs insulation and ventilation designed for the equipment load, not for a person sitting still, because the equipment runs continuously and the room is small. And the glazing has to be arranged so the operator can see the plate and the approach without leaving the seat, which usually means a corner window rather than a single one on the traffic side.

Access for the operator is the other constraint: a door that opens onto the traffic lane is a hazard, and a door that opens away from it means the operator crosses the lane to reach the office. The arrangement that works puts the office on an island between lanes, with steps that no vehicle can reach.

5. Power, data and the barrier that always arrives later

Almost every weighbridge gains equipment after it is built: a second camera, an automatic barrier, a radio link, a remote display, a card reader.

Each of those needs power and data at a point that was not in the original drawing, and each is installed by drilling the building. A small duct bank, put in during construction and left with spare capacity, costs very little and prevents a wall from becoming a patchwork.

The barrier is the one to plan for specifically. It needs a foundation, a power supply and a loop or sensor in the road surface, and retrofitting one into a finished hardstanding means cutting the slab where trucks brake. Leaving a duct and a foundation position under the canopy is the cheapest decision on the project.

6. What I want before drawings

  • The vehicle types, their lengths and the weighing method — one pass or two.
  • The dispatch rate, so the number of bridges and lanes comes from arithmetic.
  • Where the queue may stand, and where it must not.
  • Who steps out of the office, and onto what.
  • Whether a barrier, cameras or a second bridge are likely later.
  • The wind speed to be used, taken from the applicable code at the project site rather than a round number, plus the canopy's open-edge detail — a large, thin roof on slender columns over an exposed hardstanding is loaded by uplift as much as by weight, and the fixings at the open edges are where a storm finds it first.

A weighbridge is a traffic building with a scale in it. Design the approach, the queue and the impact, and the small room on the island takes care of itself.
